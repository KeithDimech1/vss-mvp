Summary
Keith Dimech, Wayne Noble, Fabian Kohlmann, Vinko Novak, and Moritz Theile discussed the Lith Surfer product tiers (Free, Pro, Enterprise) and the distinction between publicly available, commercial, proprietary, and private data types, emphasizing the use of Lith Surfer Pro/Enterprise for viewing private data alongside all other data. They focused on the future strategy, pricing, and project selection for Lith Build, with Vinko Novak differentiating between "contract for work" (fixed price) and "contract for services" (time and material), while Fabian Kohlmann and Wayne Noble stressed that ideal projects should bring in data and that the company should retain the Intellectual Property (IP). The participants also outlined three categories of build projects (outside the system, on top of Lith Surfer, and platform changes), defined criteria for assessing projects (IP risk, data access, new tools, and ethics), and discussed the need for new data models (borehole, structural, isotope, and heavy mineral) and enterprise-level services like single sign-on (SSO).

* Discussion on Lith Surfer product tiers and data types: Keith Dimech summarized the discussion around Lith Surfer's different tiers: Free, Pro, and Enterprise. The Free tier remains the same with no required changes. The Pro tier is an institute license, limiting access to one institute and one project for unshareable data. The Enterprise tier allows for multiple projects or institutes under the company's community. The data types discussed include Lith Surfer's free data from EarthBank, which is publicly available. Commercial data, which is other people's data sold via the marketplace, can be purchased either as a subscription or a one-off purchase. The proprietary data, or "our data," will be sold as a subscription. Private data is a third type that customers can add through Lith Surfer Pro or Enterprise to view in relation to all other data (commercial, subscription, or free) on the platform.

* Contract types and pricing for Lith Build projects: Vinko Novak, drawing on their experience, distinguished between a "contract for work" (fixed price) and a "contract for services" (time and material). Vinko Novak suggested that fixed-price contracts are suitable for building standardized systems (e.g., platform XYZ for a set price), while time and material contracts are better for individual, specific needs where the scope is less certain. Vinko Novak explained that time and material contracts often use different pricing for different roles, with very senior staff, like someone who defines architecture and understands requirements (e.g., "Morates"), being more expensive than just developers. The daily rate for a mixed calculation was cited as potentially €2,000. Keith Dimech noted that the past projects aligned with the "systems one" (fixed price lump sum) but envisions future Lith Build potentially involving smaller, ongoing rate work as part of an enterprise agreement. Keith Dimech also mentioned that typical consulting contracts break down rates by seniority, with senior management having a higher rate but less time on the contract, and juniors having a lower rate but essentially full-time involvement. Keith Dimech mentioned a common industry metric for day rate calculation, which is 4.5 times what the person is paid, although they acknowledged this often leads to overpayment for consultants.
* Risks and responsibilities related to contract types: Vinko Novak emphasized the difference in responsibility between contract types: in a contract for work, the company is responsible for the entire solution, including fixing delays or non-working issues, while with time and material, the customer is responsible and instructs the company. Vinko Novak prefers time and material contracts. Wayne Noble suggested that time and materials work best for a "trusted organization" with a track record, while those without a track record might face client resistance to the risk compared to a fixed cost. Keith Dimech mentioned the Ammyra contract as an example of a system contract that lacks clarity on deliverables, making it a risk. Keith Dimech recommended clearly setting out deliverables in system contracts so that any client dissatisfaction beyond those deliverables is a clear variation, which could then transition to a time and material contract.
